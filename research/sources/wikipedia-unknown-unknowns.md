"**There are unknown unknowns**" is a phrase from a response [United States Secretary of Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Secretary_of_Defense "United States Secretary of Defense") [Donald Rumsfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Rumsfeld "Donald Rumsfeld") gave to a question at a [U.S. Department of Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Department_of_Defense "U.S. Department of Defense") (DoD) news briefing on February 12, 2002, about the lack of evidence linking the government of [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba'athist_Iraq "Ba'athist Iraq") with the supply of [weapons of mass destruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weapons_of_mass_destruction "Weapons of mass destruction") to terrorist groups.[\[1\]](#cite_note-defense.gov-transcript-1) Rumsfeld stated:

> Reports that say that something hasn't happened are always interesting to me, because as we know, there are known knowns; there are things we know we know. We also know there are known unknowns; that is to say we know there are some things we do not know. But there are also unknown unknowns—the ones we don't know we don't know. And if one looks throughout the history of our country and other free countries, it is the latter category that tends to be the difficult ones.[\[1\]](#cite_note-defense.gov-transcript-1)

The statement became the subject of much commentary. In *The Decision Book* (2013), author [Mikael Krogerus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikael_Krogerus?action=edit&redlink=1 "Mikael Krogerus (page does not exist)") \[[de](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikael%20Krogerus "de:Mikael Krogerus")\] refers to it as the "Rumsfeld matrix".[\[2\]](#cite_note-2) The statement also features in a 2013 documentary film, *[The Unknown Known](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Unknown_Known "The Unknown Known")*, directed by [Errol Morris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errol_Morris "Errol Morris").[\[3\]](#cite_note-GirardGirard2009-3)

Known unknowns refers to "risks you are aware of, such as canceled flights",[\[4\]](#cite_note-Biafore-4) whereas unknown unknowns are risks that come from situations that are so unexpected that they would not be considered.

Rumsfeld's statement brought attention to the concepts of known knowns, known unknowns, and unknown unknowns, but these were in common use in US defense procurement by the late 1960s. In a 1968 study sponsored by the Aerospace Industries Association, Hudson Drake from North American Rockwell argued that defence contractors had to solve both known unknowns and "unanticipated unknowns".[\[5\]](#cite_note-5) Also in 1968, Lt. Gen. William B. Bunker noted that when developing complex weapons systems "there are two kinds of technical problems: there are the known unknowns, and the unknown unknowns."[\[6\]](#cite_note-6) The usage was common enough for an industry shorthand to have developed where unknown-unknowns were referred to as "unk-unks".[\[7\]](#cite_note-7)

The term was commonly used inside [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA "NASA").[\[8\]](#cite_note-8) Rumsfeld cited [NASA administrator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA_administrator "NASA administrator") [William Graham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Robert_Graham "William Robert Graham") in his memoir; he wrote that he had first heard "a variant of the phrase" from Graham when they served together on the [Commission to Assess the Ballistic Missile Threat to the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commission_to_Assess_the_Ballistic_Missile_Threat_to_the_United_States "Commission to Assess the Ballistic Missile Threat to the United States") during the late 1990s.[\[9\]](#cite_note-Rumsfeld1-9) Rumsfeld had previously publicly used the terms himself, stating in a 2000 speech that "There are known knowns, known unknowns and unknown unknowns. Effective intelligence work must consider them all."[\[10\]](#cite_note-10)

The terms "known unknowns" and "unknown unknowns" are often used in [project management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_management "Project management") and [strategic planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_planning "Strategic planning")[\[11\]](#cite_note-11) circles.

Contemporary usage is largely consistent with the earliest known usages. For example, the term was used in evidence given to the British Columbia Royal Commission of Inquiry into Uranium Mining in 1979:

> Site conditions always pose unknowns, or uncertainties, which may become known during construction or operation to the detriment of the facility and possibly lead to damage of the environment or endanger public health and safety. The risk posed by unknowns is somewhat dependent on the nature of the unknown relative to past experience. This has led me to classify unknowns into one of the following two types:
>
> 1\. known unknowns (expected or foreseeable conditions), which can be reasonably anticipated but not quantified based on past experience as exemplified by case histories (in Appendix A) and
>
> 2\. Unknown unknowns (unexpected or unforeseeable conditions), which pose a potentially greater risk simply because they cannot be anticipated based on past experience or investigation.
>
> Known unknowns result from recognized but poorly understood phenomena. On the other hand, unknown unknowns are phenomena which cannot be expected because there has been no prior experience or theoretical basis for expecting the phenomena.[\[12\]](#cite_note-12)

The term also appeared in a 1982 *[New Yorker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Yorker "The New Yorker")* article on the [aerospace industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerospace_industry "Aerospace industry"), which cites the example of [metal fatigue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal_fatigue "Metal fatigue"), the cause of crashes in [de Havilland Comet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Havilland_Comet "De Havilland Comet") airliners in the 1950s.[\[13\]](#cite_note-13)

Canadian columnist [Mark Steyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Steyn "Mark Steyn") called it "in fact a brilliant distillation of quite a complex matter".[\[14\]](#cite_note-telegraph-14) Australian economist and blogger [John Quiggin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Quiggin "John Quiggin") wrote: "Although the language may be tortured, the basic point is both valid and important."[\[15\]](#cite_note-15)

Psychoanalytic philosopher [Slavoj Žižek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavoj_Žižek "Slavoj Žižek") says that beyond these three categories there is a fourth, the unknown known, that which one intentionally refuses to acknowledge that one knows: "If Rumsfeld thinks that the main dangers in the confrontation with Iraq were the 'unknown unknowns', that is, the threats from Saddam whose nature we cannot even suspect, then the [Abu Ghraib scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Ghraib_torture_and_prisoner_abuse "Abu Ghraib torture and prisoner abuse") shows that the main dangers lie in the 'unknown knowns'—the disavowed beliefs, suppositions and obscene practices we pretend not to know about, even though they form the background of our public values."[\[16\]](#cite_note-16)

German sociologists Christopher Daase and Oliver Kessler agreed that the [cognitive frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framing_\(sociology\) "Framing (sociology)") for political practice may be determined by the relationship between "what we know, what we do not know, what we cannot know", but stated that Rumsfeld left out "what we do not like to know".[\[17\]](#cite_note-kauk-17)

The event has been used in multiple books to discuss [risk assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk_assessment "Risk assessment").[\[3\]](#cite_note-GirardGirard2009-3)[\[18\]](#cite_note-NeveLuetchford2008-18)

Rumsfeld named his 2011 autobiography *[Known and Unknown: A Memoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Known_and_Unknown:_A_Memoir "Known and Unknown: A Memoir")*. In an author's note at the start of the book, he expressly acknowledges the source of his memoir's title and mentions a few examples of his statement's prominence.[\[19\]](#cite_note-Rumsfeld2-19) *[The Unknown Known](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Unknown_Known "The Unknown Known")* is the title of [Errol Morris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errol_Morris "Errol Morris")'s 2013 biographical documentary film about Rumsfeld.[\[20\]](#cite_note-20) In it, Rumsfeld initially defines "unknown knowns" as "the things you think you know, that it turns out you did not", and toward the end of the film he re-defines the term as "things that you know, that you don't know you know".[\[21\]](#cite_note-21)

Rumsfeld's comment earned the 2003 [Foot in Mouth Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot_in_Mouth_Award "Foot in Mouth Award") from the British [Plain English Campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plain_English_Campaign "Plain English Campaign").[\[22\]](#cite_note-22)

In April 2003, *[Slate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slate_\(magazine\) "Slate (magazine)")* columnist Hart Seely arranged Rumsfeld's public statements as [found poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Found_poetry "Found poetry"), presenting the "unknown unknowns" passage as a poem titled "The Unknown".[\[23\]](#cite_note-23) Seely later published a full collection, *Pieces of Intelligence: The Existential Poetry of Donald H. Rumsfeld* (2003).[\[24\]](#cite_note-24) The poems were subsequently set to music by pianist Bryant Kong and performed by soprano Elender Wall.[\[25\]](#cite_note-25)

As alluded to by Rumsfeld in his autobiography,[\[19\]](#cite_note-Rumsfeld2-19): xv [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece") philosopher [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates "Socrates") considered known unknowns and unknown unknowns.[\[26\]](#cite_note-26)[\[27\]](#cite_note-27) Much later, philosopher and theologian [Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas "Thomas Aquinas") in his *[Summa Theologica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_Theologica "Summa Theologica")* also emphasized the important difference between recognized ignorance and unconscious ignorance.[\[28\]](#cite_note-28)

Rumsfeld's statement closely parallelled a [well-known proverb about knowledge](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Knowledge#Proverbs "q:Knowledge"):[\[29\]](#cite_note-29)[\[30\]](#cite_note-Saravanan2021-30)

> He who knows not, and knows not that he knows not, is a fool. Shun him.
> He who knows not, and knows that he knows not, is simple. Teach him.
> He who knows, and knows not that he knows, is asleep. Wake him.
> He who knows, and knows that he knows, is wise. Follow him.

This has been widely quoted[\[31\]](#cite_note-31) since the 19th century as (for example) an anonymous Persian,[\[32\]](#cite_note-32)[\[33\]](#cite_note-33) Arabic,[\[34\]](#cite_note-34)[\[35\]](#cite_note-35) African,[\[36\]](#cite_note-36) Japanese,[\[37\]](#cite_note-37) Oriental[\[38\]](#cite_note-38) or simply an old[\[39\]](#cite_note-39) proverb, or attributed to authors ranging from [Confucius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confucius "Confucius")[\[40\]](#cite_note-40) to [Bruce Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Lee "Bruce Lee").[\[41\]](#cite_note-41) The proverb is actually a close translation (with line order reversed) of [al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khalil_ibn_Ahmad_al-Farahidi "Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi")'s medieval epigram about the "four kinds of men", as reported by [Al-Ghazali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ghazali "Al-Ghazali") (1058-1111 AD),[\[42\]](#cite_note-42)[\[43\]](#cite_note-43) which was later echoed in poems by [Nasir al-Din al-Tusi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasir_al-Din_al-Tusi "Nasir al-Din al-Tusi")[\[44\]](#cite_note-44) and [Ibn Yamin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Yamin#Categorization_of_knowledge_and_ignorance "Ibn Yamin").[\[45\]](#cite_note-45)

"Unknown unknowns" were occasionally mentioned in the 1950s and 1960s. In 1950, it was noted that sociology research was full of "unknown unknowns".[\[46\]](#cite_note-46) In a 1962 commencement address, Nobel laureate biochemist [Melvin Calvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melvin_Calvin "Melvin Calvin") discussed how humanity "must grapple not only with the known and the 'known unknown', but also with the vastness of the 'unknown unknown'."[\[47\]](#cite_note-47)

A related 2x2 grid was created in 1955 by two American psychologists, Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham in their development of the [Johari window](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johari_window "Johari window"), a "graphic model of interpersonal behaviour"[\[48\]](#cite_note-48) that classifies knowledge about your behavior and motivations in terms of whether you or others are aware of those behaviours or motivations. For example, your motivation might be (un)known by you and (un)known by others. Another similar[\[49\]](#cite_note-49) classification scheme is the [conscious competence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_stages_of_competence "Four stages of competence") learning model published in 1960, where a person's knowledge and skills are classified according to how (un)conscious and (in)competent they are.[\[50\]](#cite_note-50)

## Analytical sciences

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=There_are_unknown_unknowns&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Analytical sciences")\]

The term "known unknowns" has been applied to the identification of chemical substances using [analytical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_chemistry "Analytical chemistry") approaches, specifically [mass spectrometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_spectrometry "Mass spectrometry"). In many cases, an unknown to an investigator that is detected in an experiment is actually known in the chemical literature, a reference database, or an Internet resource. These types of compounds are termed "known unknowns". The term was originally coined by Little et al.[\[51\]](#cite_note-51) and reported a number of times in the literature since then as a general approach.[\[52\]](#cite_note-52)[\[53\]](#cite_note-53)[\[54\]](#cite_note-54)[\[55\]](#cite_note-55)
