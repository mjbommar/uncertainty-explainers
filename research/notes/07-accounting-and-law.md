# 7. Accounting and law

Accounting standards and courts both have to act on facts nobody can yet see.
Each has built a small, fixed vocabulary of likelihood words and tied each word
to an action. None of the sources below turns those words into numbers, with
one exception: the tax rule in FIN 48 (now ASC 740) says "greater than 50
percent" outright.

## Key claims

- FAS 5 (now ASC 450) splits the range of likelihood of a loss into three named areas: probable, reasonably possible, and remote. [S71]
- The three words are defined by other words, not numbers: probable means "likely to occur"; reasonably possible means "more than remote but less than likely"; remote means "slight". [S71]
- Each word triggers a different action. Probable plus a loss that can be reasonably estimated means the company books the loss (accrual). Reasonably possible means the company tells readers in a note (disclosure) but books nothing. Remote means neither. [S71, S70]
- Two conditions must both hold before a loss is booked: the loss is probable, and its amount can be reasonably estimated. [S70, S71]
- FAS 5 banned "reserves for general contingencies", that is, cushions set aside for vague business risk with no specific event behind them. [S70]
- The worked example in FAS 5: a company that will probably owe $2 million in tax, and might owe $8 million more, books the $2 million and discloses the extra exposure if it is reasonably possible. [S71]
- FAS 157 (now ASC 820) ranks how a fair value was reached by how much of it rests on market evidence (observable inputs) versus the company's own assumptions (unobservable inputs). [S72]
- Level 1 is a quoted price in an active market, times the quantity held. Level 3 is a measurement that uses significant unobservable inputs, and it gets extra disclosure. [S72]
- SOP 94-6 (now ASC 275) requires a warning when it is at least reasonably possible that an estimate will change in the "near term", defined as within one year. [S74]
- SOP 94-6 declares some events reasonably possible by rule: losing a major customer, and disruption of operations outside the home country. [S74]
- FIN 48 (now ASC 740) uses a two-step test for a tax position: first, is it "more likely than not" to survive examination? If so, book the largest amount that is "greater than 50 percent likely" to be realized. [S73]
- In Addington v. Texas (1979), the Supreme Court said a standard of proof tells the factfinder how confident society wants it to be, and decides how the risk of error is split between the parties. [S75]
- The Court names three levels on a continuum: preponderance of the evidence (ordinary civil cases), clear and convincing evidence (the middle level), and beyond a reasonable doubt (criminal cases). [S75]
- The Court admits it does not know how jurors actually apply these phrases, and that it found no study comparing all three. [S75]

## Definitions in plain words

- **Contingency**: a situation that exists today whose money result depends on a future event that may or may not happen. [S71]
- **Accrue**: record the loss in the accounts now, as a charge against income. [S71]
- **Disclose**: describe the risk in the notes to the financial statements without recording a number in the accounts. [S71]
- **Observable input**: a figure taken from market data that the company does not control. **Unobservable input**: the company's own assumption about what the market would use. [S72]
- **Near term**: within one year of the date of the financial statements (SOP 94-6). [S74]
- **Severe impact**: an effect that seriously disrupts the entity's normal operations. It is more than material but can be less than catastrophic. [S74]
- **More likely than not**: FIN 48 applies it as a threshold, and measures the recognized benefit as the largest amount that is "greater than 50 percent likely" to be realized. [S73]
- **Standard of proof**: the degree of confidence a factfinder must reach before finding a fact true. [S75]
- **Risk of error**: the chance that the decision will be wrong. The standard of proof decides which side bears most of that chance. [S75]

## Tables

### ASC 450 (FAS 5): likelihood word to required action

| Likelihood word | FAS 5 definition | Amount can be reasonably estimated | Amount cannot be estimated |
|---|---|---|---|
| Probable | "likely to occur" | Accrue (book the loss), and disclose its nature if needed | Disclose; do not accrue |
| Reasonably possible | "more than remote but less than likely" | Disclose; do not accrue | Disclose; state that an estimate cannot be made |
| Remote | "slight" | Nothing required | Nothing required |

Source: paragraphs 3, 8, 10, and 37 to 38 of FAS 5 as reprinted by the PCAOB [S71]; summary [S70]. A special case: for an unasserted claim (no one has yet claimed anything), disclosure is required only if assertion is probable and an unfavorable outcome is reasonably possible. [S71]

### ASC 820 (FAS 157): fair value hierarchy

| Level | What our source says | Plain words |
|---|---|---|
| Level 1 | A position in an instrument trading in an active market "should be measured as the product of the quoted price for the individual instrument times the quantity held" | Look up the price on the exchange |
| Level 2 | Not described in the FASB summary we fetched | (Gap: in ASC 820, inputs that are observable but are not quoted prices for the identical item. Our sources do not state this, so do not use it on screen without a new source.) |
| Level 3 | Measurements "using significant unobservable inputs", with extra disclosure of their effect on earnings | Mostly the company's own model and assumptions |

Source: [S72].

### Standards of proof (Addington v. Texas, 1979)

| Word | Where used | What the Court said |
|---|---|---|
| Preponderance of the evidence | Typical civil case, money disputes between private parties | "The litigants thus share the risk of error in roughly equal fashion." |
| Clear and convincing evidence | Middle level; required for involuntary civil commitment to a mental hospital | The proof "must be greater than the preponderance-of-the-evidence standard applicable to other categories of civil cases"; 20 states used this wording by statute or case law |
| Beyond a reasonable doubt | Criminal cases (and juvenile delinquency, per In re Winship) | "our society imposes almost the entire risk of error upon itself"; the standard is "a compromise between what is possible to prove and what protects the rights of the individual" |

Source: [S75].

## Numbers and stories for the screen

- **The $2 million and the $8 million.** A company in a tax lawsuit will probably owe $2 million and might owe $8 million more. It books $2 million and writes a note about the $8 million. Same lawsuit, two likelihood words, two different treatments. [S71]
- **Three words, three buckets, three actions.** Probable leads to book it; reasonably possible leads to tell readers; remote leads to nothing. A good sorting animation. [S71]
- **The one rule that says a number.** FIN 48 is the only source here that states a percentage: "greater than 50 percent likely". [S73]
- **Risk of error as a see-saw.** Civil case: the risk is shared "in roughly equal fashion". Criminal case: society takes "almost the entire risk of error upon itself". [S75]
- **The Court's candor.** The Supreme Court said the way jurors actually use these phrases "may well be unknowable" and that it found no study comparing all three standards. [S75]
- **Probable by decree.** SOP 94-6 does not ask whether losing a big customer is reasonably possible. It declares that it always is. [S74]
- **One year.** SOP 94-6 fixes the "near term" at one year from the statement date, a rare case of a hard number in this vocabulary. [S74]

## Gaps and cautions

- **No numeric thresholds in FAS 5 or Addington.** Common glosses such as "probable is about 70 to 80 percent" or "preponderance is 51 percent" do not appear in any source here. Do not put them on screen as rules. If a video uses them, it must label them as practitioner conventions and cite a new source. [S71, S75]
- **The only explicit percentage is in FIN 48.** Its "greater than 50 percent" applies to measuring the benefit amount. The summary uses "more likely than not" for the recognition threshold without separately defining it in numbers. [S73]
- **"Probable" means different things in different standards.** Our sources show only the FAS 5 meaning ("likely to occur"). We have no source on how IFRS uses the word, so we cannot claim a contrast.
- **ASC 820 Level 2 is not described** in the FASB summary we fetched [S72]. A new source is needed before Level 2 is defined on screen.
- **SOP 94-6 is secondary.** S74 is a 1995 CPA Journal article that quotes the SOP's definitions. The primary SOP and the ASC 275 text were blocked by captchas at every fetch tier. Quote S74 as reporting the SOP.
- **These are pre-codification sources.** FAS 5, FAS 157, FIN 48, and SOP 94-6 were later folded into ASC 450, 820, 740, and 275. The pairing is stated in our research register, not in these source texts. Present-day ASC wording may differ.
- **Addington is about civil commitment.** Its account of the three standards is general, but its holding applies to involuntary commitment proceedings. [S75]
